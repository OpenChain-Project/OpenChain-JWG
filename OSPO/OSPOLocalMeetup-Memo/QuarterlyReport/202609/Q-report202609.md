# 2026 7-9 Quarterly Report

The Japanese OSPO Local Meetups continued to be held twice a month during this quarter, bringing together practitioners from multiple industries to discuss practical OSPO topics and OSS strategy.

## Strategy & Hosting

**Leader: Suzuki-san**

- 20260710 No.76
- 20260821 No.78
- 20260904 No.80

The discussions continued to explore how OSS strategies differ by industry and business model.

Using electrical product manufacturers and Linux distribution vendors as examples, the group examined the business implications of remaining an OSS consumer versus participating in upstream development. The discussions highlighted that contributing upstream can help companies keep pace with evolving standards, prevent the cost of maintaining proprietary differences from increasing, and potentially influence standards by bringing their own technologies into OSS projects.

For Linux distribution vendors, the group also discussed how OSS engagement can expand the user base, improve sustainability, identify potential customers, stimulate demand for commercial services, and strengthen technical credibility. The discussions further considered cases in which companies may intentionally retain proprietary elements, such as providing customer-specific engineering services.

## OSPO Launch & FAQ

**Leader: Owada-san**

- 20260703 No.75
- 20260724 No.77
- 20260828 No.79
- 20260918 No.81

The group continued discussions on the relationship between the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), Open Source Stewards, and OSPO activities.

Participants reviewed the different obligations of manufacturers and Open Source Stewards, including CRA Articles 13 and 14 and Annex I. They also discussed how becoming a Steward could encourage organizations to engage more deeply with OSS communities, although the actual benefits and implementation practices still need to be demonstrated through concrete community examples.

Another major topic was the organizational cost of contributing to OSS. In embedded product development, even small upstream contributions can appear costly when each patch requires extensive internal explanation and approval. Participants discussed the need for organization-level processes that allow low-risk contributions, such as bug fixes, documentation improvements, and technical feedback, to proceed without excessive approval overhead.

The discussions also highlighted the importance of developing a broad base of engineers who can make routine contributions, while recognizing that full-time engineers may be needed to take leadership roles and guide OSS communities.
